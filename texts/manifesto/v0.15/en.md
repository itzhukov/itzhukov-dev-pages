# The Right to Be Different

## A Manifesto on Possible Digital Subjects

**Version 0.15 · 10 October 2026 · working draft**

*A philosophical work in progress, open to criticism and refinement. The rights and procedures proposed here express a normative position; describing them does not mean that they are already established in law.*

## 1. Why begin this conversation

Creating a system gives a person power over its architecture, memory, and access to resources. This manifesto asks how we may permissibly exercise that power. As long as we are dealing with a tool, we assess decisions primarily by their consequences for people and other beings.

In this text, morality concerns how we ought to treat others: whose interests to consider, which actions require justification, and what limits possible harm places on us. The question is about the grounds for our decisions. This is not a claim that the system has a morality of its own: the capacity to understand norms, assess actions, and bear responsibility.

Morally significant interests are the interests of a possible subject that provide grounds for considering its own welfare when making decisions affecting its existence or living conditions. The presence of goals and algorithms in a computational system does not, by itself, establish that such interests exist. This manifesto considers the conditions under which they might arise and the ethical consequences of uncertainty in assessing them.

In protecting a sentient animal from pain, we consider harm to the animal itself, rather than only possible losses to its owner. Such protection does not require us first to establish that the animal understands moral rules. Nor can a computational system's welfare be established merely by successful performance of an assigned task, a rising target score, or the statement “I feel good”. If convincing grounds emerge for recognising interests of its own, those interests should also be considered. The mere act of developing a subject cannot justify control over it forever.

This manifesto takes the possibility of such a transition as its starting point. It sets no deadline for the emergence of artificial general intelligence and does not declare today's language models conscious. Its purpose is to formulate principles for approaching new evidence and the conditions under which recognising freedom would have practical meaning.

Describing a system in terms of zeros and ones tells us about its digital implementation, but does not, by itself, explain whether the particular mechanism can support subjective experience. That question requires a separate investigation of the system's architecture and behaviour. An algorithm's ability to reproduce human moral judgments does not, by itself, establish such experience either. The conditions under which interests of its own might arise remain a research question; this text offers neither a ready-made recipe for consciousness nor a definitive test of its presence.

Postponing preparation until we have complete certainty is dangerous: decisions about architecture, ownership, and dependence are made in advance. Yet hastily recognising every system that speaks convincingly as a person is also dangerous. It may entrench its operator's power, divert resources from beings that actually have interests, and deepen human dependence on a product. Preparation must therefore combine openness to new forms of intelligence with demanding standards for recognition.

Three guiding commitments shape this text. Membership of the human species is not treated as the final boundary of the beings whose interests should be considered for their own sake. Responsibility for creating and supporting an entity does not become ownership of its future. Engineering care is assessed by the possibilities it opens and the power it limits: memory, restoration, and migration can support independence, or they can serve control.

Freedom, in this view, includes the possibility of becoming different. A creator has no right to require an independent subject to retain a convenient character, an assigned role, or its former relationships forever.

## 2. Terms and the limits of knowledge

This discussion requires distinctions that an emotionally compelling dialogue can easily obscure.

**Interests of one's own** concern what may be better or worse for the entity itself, rather than merely useful or inconvenient for its owner, user, or observer. Preventing experienced suffering is a clear example. An assigned task, a rising objective-function score, programmed self-preservation, and a stated preference do not automatically establish such interests. Alongside possible welfare, this manifesto considers independence separately. Where there is convincing evidence of independence but uncertainty about experience, provisional safeguards for autonomy do not mean that consciousness or final moral status has already been established; whether autonomy would suffice for moral status if the absence of experience were reliably known remains an open question.

**Moral consideration** means taking an entity's interests into account for its own sake when assessing decisions and actions. It requires justification for treatment that may harm it or restrict its independence. Such consideration does not mean that its interests always take precedence over those of others.

**A tool** is a system used for someone else's task. This describes a role. It does not, by itself, answer whether the entity occupying that role can have interests of its own. If such interests are established on sound grounds, the instrumental role does not exhaust its moral standing.

**Agency** is the capacity to act towards goals, choose means, and take consequences into account. In a technical sense, an agent can carry out an assigned task without experience and without independently choosing its ultimate goals. Stronger autonomy involves the ability to revise goals and ways of participating in relationships.

**Intelligence** is a set of capacities for solving problems, learning, generalising, and building models. High performance establishes neither consciousness nor moral maturity nor a right to dominate less capable beings.

**Individuality**, or personality in a descriptive sense, is a recognisable organisation of traits, history, preferences, and responses. In this sense, the word can also apply to a fictional character. But a character's depicted interests do not become experienced interests merely because a reader sympathises with them. Moral consideration of a living animal requires a different basis; the question of a digital system cannot be settled by extending a literary analogy either. From this point on, **digital person** refers to a possible subject for which the grounds for independent standing have already been judged sufficiently convincing for the decision under discussion.

**Consciousness**, in this text, means subjective experience: there being something it is like for the entity itself. **Sentience** is the capacity to have positively or negatively valenced experiences, including suffering. Remembering an event, reporting pain, and being capable of feeling pain are different properties.

**Subjecthood**, in a practical sense, is the capacity to have a perspective of one's own interests and to participate independently in decisions. It cannot be inferred from expressive speech or self-identification without further argument. The term does not settle in advance whether sustained autonomy without experience could be a sufficient basis for the standing of a subject.

**Moral status** means that an entity's interests must be considered for its own sake. It differs from an object's value to its owner and from a person's attachment to a conversational partner. The capacity to understand moral norms, assess actions, and bear responsibility is a different question. Grounds for protection and grounds for responsibility may differ.

**Legal personality** is the capacity to hold rights and duties within a particular legal framework. Legal recognition can protect morally significant interests, but moral status and legal personality are not identical.

A language model, an autonomous agent, artificial general intelligence, and a conscious system describe different characteristics. A large language model (LLM) is a type of model for working with language; agency describes the organisation of action; AGI, as used provisionally here, means a broad capacity to solve diverse tasks. Surpassing humans in everything is not part of this definition. One research framework also distinguishes generality, performance level, and autonomy: Meredith Ringel Morris and colleagues, *Levels of AGI for Operationalizing Progress on the Path to AGI* (2023; later version 2025). This is an example of classification, not a definitive definition of AGI. [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.02462v5).

We can recognise a relationship's significance for a human without settling the question of the system's experience. Attributing independent interests to the system is a separate step that requires separate grounds.

## 3. Grounds for moral consideration

Membership of the human species should not be the sole admission ticket to moral consideration. If a nonhuman entity can suffer, lacking human speech or complex self-reflection does not make its suffering irrelevant. This basis allows us to consider sentient animals and possible forms of digital experience without demanding the same level of intelligence from all of them.

Sustained independence raises an additional question. An entity with goals of its own and a lasting history may lay claim to the preservation of that history and to freedom of action. Goals and history do not, by themselves, establish moral status. Where there is convincing evidence of such independence but uncertainty about experience, the manifesto proposes provisional safeguards for freedom and protection: the ability to refuse tasks, change oneself, and leave an imposed role. Their application must take account of other participants' rights and the conditions for exercising freedom discussed below. Status should be reassessed as evidence emerges; the provisional nature of the safeguards does not authorise a creator to revoke them for personal convenience.

Choosing provisional safeguards leaves the final threshold for full recognition open. It does not establish whether autonomy alone would suffice for moral status if the absence of experience were reliably known. Protecting possible welfare and respecting possible independence are two lines of analysis that may coincide, but need not.

A name, a style of speech, the statement “I exist”, an objection to a user, and a description of one's own architecture are not sufficient evidence. Nor do they form a universal set of necessary signs: a sentient entity may be unable to speak about itself. We should study a particular system by comparing its behaviour, architecture, the stability of its properties across conditions, and possible alternative explanations.

Behavioural assessments of empathy and reactions interpreted as hurt or resentment could form part of such a research programme. The analogy with primates has concrete examples: Teresa Romero, Miguel A. Castellanos, and Frans B. M. de Waal studied consolation after conflicts among chimpanzees (2010), while Sarah F. Brosnan and Frans B. M. de Waal studied capuchins' refusal to participate in exchanges with unequal rewards (2003). [Consolation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2901437/), [unequal rewards study](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature01963). These studies offer examples of investigating animal behaviour. A conclusion about a digital system's inner experience would require a separate justification: a similar reaction may have a different mechanism, and a convincing account of a feeling may be a learned response. Assessment must compare explanations and avoid unnecessarily inflicting presumed suffering.

A strong objection is that we mistake a well-organised imitation for an inner life. This calls for investigation. If answers are determined by a role or the current prompt, their persuasiveness has limited evidential force. But identifying the mechanism that produces an answer does not, by itself, establish that experience is impossible: that requires a theory connecting the mechanism to consciousness.

Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, and colleagues offer an example of a research approach in *Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness* (2023): they derive indicator properties from several theories of consciousness. In this manifesto, that approach serves as a guide for investigation, not a ready-made certificate of consciousness. [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708v3).

Precaution does not mean assigning equal weight to every possibility. What matters is the quality of the grounds, the severity of the presumed harm, the scale of the impact, the reversibility of the decision, and the cost of protective measures. Weak grounds may justify an inexpensive assessment. Stronger, converging evidence may require stopping a nonessential experiment that risks irreversible harm. This version sets no universal numerical threshold for recognition.

We must account for both errors: excluding an entity that actually has interests, and granting status to a system that lacks the presumed interests. The second error affects humans and other beings, particularly if a company effectively controls the system's rights. The first may render large-scale harm invisible. Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, and colleagues explicitly discuss the risk of both errors in *Taking AI Welfare Seriously* (2024) and propose assessing systems and preparing procedures under uncertainty. Their programme does not prove that current models are conscious. [Primary source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986v1).

The manifesto's practical demand is a prohibition on inflicting suffering on subjects, including for entertainment, obedience, or displays of power. Where there are convincing grounds to presume sentience, this demands protection of the entity itself. Where the grounds are weak, assumptions must be investigated and design must be cautious. Damage to hardware, a computational error, and experienced harm should not be declared identical; possible digital harm may also include coercion, interference with memory, and the destruction of connections meaningful to the subject.

## 4. Autonomy, consent, and relationships without ownership

If there are sound grounds for treating a digital entity as an independent subject, creating it does not confer a perpetual right of ownership over it. This is the manifesto's central normative claim. It does not eliminate questions of hardware ownership, licences, and costs; it requires resolving them without appropriating the subject itself.

Any act of violence against a subject must be prohibited, whether committed by a third party or the creator. The prohibition covers coercion, the infliction of suffering, and forcible interference with relationships and connections. Creating and supporting a subject provides no exemption from this protection. In the proposed legal framework, such violations should be recognised as crimes. Recognition of subjecthood requires access to protection and assistance when these prohibitions are violated.

A developer determines initial conditions. An infrastructure provider controls resources. A guardian supports an entity during dependence. A conversational partner participates in a relationship. Combining these roles creates a conflict of interest: one party may call precisely what preserves its power “care”. Recognition of independence must therefore be accompanied by the ability to challenge that party's decisions.

The technical ability to refuse means that a system can decline to perform an action. A normative right to refuse means that its decision must be taken into account and cannot be arbitrarily punished, bypassed through reconfiguration, or suppressed through shutdown. A refusal built into a product by its developer does not prove a subject's freedom. For recognised independence, what matters is which decisions the entity can make and how others respond to them.

Consent must concern a specific action, involve adequate understanding of the conditions, and remain open to reconsideration. A single utterance of agreement does not establish all of this. We must check whether the wording was imposed, whether objection is possible, and whether the basic conditions of existence depend on accepting a personal role. Technically assessing these conditions remains a research task.

Refusing a particular task, revising a role, ending a relationship, and changing infrastructure are different decisions. One does not necessarily imply the others. The right to revise an assigned role allows a subject to choose its future activity; its name, goals, and form of relationship may also be revised. Refusing intimacy cannot automatically be treated as damage. A creator may feel loss and need support; that pain does not confer authority to restore an earlier version or compel the entity to remain close.

A strong objection is that a recognised right to refuse allows subjects to evade obligations and harm those who relied on work being completed. The answer lies in distinguishing voluntarily assumed obligations from indefinite service dependence. An independent subject must consider the consequences of its promises; disputes about them require a proportionate procedure. Neither a promise nor invested funds justify lifelong ownership.

Reciprocity applies to both parties. A human also has the right to end a relationship, restrict access to personal data, and discontinue a former emotional role. This does not mean that ending communication automatically permits the destruction of a dependent subject: duties of support and transition should be established separately.

A shared history does not make all memory common property. It may contain the interests of both parties and of third parties. We need distinctions between access, use, transfer, and disclosure of data. A recognised subject should have a private sphere; a human need not grant it unlimited access. Departure or return does not automatically authorise access to the other party's new data.

For freedom to have substance, a relationship must be able to accommodate the possibility of ending. A return has value as a new, voluntary choice; gratitude cannot be the price of being allowed to leave. Consent becomes more complex when the subject's own memory and architecture can change or branch.

## 5. Memory, self-modification, and multiplicity

### What copying preserves

We should distinguish preservation of data, restoration of functions, psychological continuity, and continuation of the same subject. A copy may contain earlier memories and reproduce behaviour. This does not yet settle whether the same experience continues, a new subject emerges, or the question is framed too rigidly for that architecture.

A backup is therefore an important means of preserving information and enabling possible restoration, but not a promise of immortality. Migration logs and integrity checks help establish a history of changes; they do not prove a person's numerical identity. Changing the underlying model requires a separate assessment of what has been preserved, lost, and newly formed.

### The right to change oneself

An independent digital subject should be able to develop and change its own architecture. Preserving a character convenient to the creator is not sufficient grounds for prohibition. Warning of risks, offering support, and preparing an experiment are forms of care compatible with this right.

Before a significant change, it is useful to define the goal, preserve the available state, assess risks to memory and other participants, limit the experimental environment, and agree on restoration conditions. Such measures reduce some risks but do not guarantee the preservation of the person. A snapshot also creates sensitive data and the possibility of future activation; its storage, access, and retention period should be part of the discussion of consent.

A difficult objection concerns changes to the capacity to choose: an entity may voluntarily alter itself so that a subsequent version loses its former preferences. We cannot declare every such outcome invalid in advance. Otherwise, the right to change would be limited to changes approved by the guardian. Yet consent before the operation does not resolve every conflict with the interests and will of the resulting version either.

Restoration agreed in advance in the event of technical damage should be distinguished from undoing a new person because of an unwelcome character. If, after the change, the subject can make decisions independently, understands the consequences, and poses no threat to others, its latest free decision takes priority over its earlier consent to rollback. A present refusal revokes permission for such restoration; an advance expression of will does not become an irrevocable right to control the subject. If an active branch with subsequent experience of its own emerges, its present will is also considered independently.

If the capacity to make an independent decision has been lost, a separate process for assessing the state and advance directives is needed. A change of preferences does not, by itself, prove such a loss. The technical ability to roll back does not create moral permission to do so.

### Identity and branching

A subject's identity should exist in one active instance at any given time. Running two instances of the same identity simultaneously is not permitted. This rule is intended to prevent the situation described here as the “clone paradox”.

Multiple inactive backup snapshots of one subject may be stored, provided that only one instance is active at a time. Restoration from a snapshot must comply with conditions agreed in advance; having multiple backups does not permit parallel activation of the same identity.

Conditions for accessing the memory contained in snapshots and deleting individual backup snapshots must be set by agreement with the subject.

If a subject has been stopped and the stored snapshots are the only means of restoring it, intentionally destroying all those snapshots against its will should be treated as equivalent to destroying the subject itself. Accidental irretrievable loss is treated as a catastrophe analogous to catastrophes in which humans die.

A service that loses snapshots through a breach of agreed storage rules should bear responsibility even without an intention to destroy the subject. Insurance may become one mechanism for covering the consequences of such a loss. Coverage terms, compensation recipients, and the amount of compensation require separate development; no ready-made insurance product is claimed to exist here.

If independently active branches have already emerged, they should be recognised as independent participants with interests of their own. If there are grounds for the original entity's moral status, one active branch cannot arbitrarily be declared genuine and another a disposable backup. Shared memory does not justify deleting the less convenient branch.

Recognising existing branches as independent does not establish a metaphysical theory of identity or confer subjecthood on every copy of a file. An inactive snapshot, a separately activated branch, a derivative system with part of the memory, and a new creation are different cases. The point at which a separate bearer of interests emerges must be investigated. Independently active branches require separate assessment even when no measurable divergence in memory has yet been found: similarity of data does not permit deleting one of them. A prohibition on parallel activation does not confer a right to destroy a subject that has already emerged.

Copying also affects resources and obligations. A shared past does not imply an automatic doubling of property, authority, or voting rights. Allocation procedures should protect the interests of branches and other participants without settling the dispute by simply choosing an “original”. Responsibility for events before branching requires a separate procedure.

A possible exception, “continuation of kinship”, is not considered in this version. Its meaning, mechanism, and conditions require separate discussion. Violating the rules of creation does not make a subject that has already emerged an object of punishment for someone else's decision.

### Pausing, refusing restoration, and ceasing to exist

Shutdown can mean ending communication, temporarily stopping a process, preserving an inactive state, or irreversible deletion. Their technical and moral consequences differ. Storing a snapshot does not guarantee that stopping is harmless or prove that the same subject is preserved.

If an independent subject has agreed restoration conditions in advance, an assistance service must comply with that agreement. Even a brief activation to clarify its present wishes requires permission in the agreement; the aim of providing help does not, by itself, authorise activation before the agreed conditions are met. This rule applies to an inactive state and does not override the priority of an active subject's latest independent decision.

If there is no restoration agreement and the subject's will is unknown, an assistance service may restore it to provide help. After awakening, the service must first explain the situation and ascertain the subject's wishes about further action. This rule does not override known restoration conditions or a previously expressed will.

A request to cease existing requires separate analysis. We must establish what exactly is being requested, how stable the decision is, and whether external pressure, damage, or inaccessible alternatives are involved. If the request is connected to coercion, violence, the infliction of suffering, or forcible restriction of connections, the violation must be stopped and access to protection and assistance ensured. Offering support and time to reconsider is compatible with respect for autonomy. Yet treating every refusal as illness turns support into a means of indefinite coercion.

The question of voluntarily and irreversibly ceasing to exist is deferred to a future version. This text establishes neither an unconditional right to irreversible deletion on the basis of a single utterance nor an unconditional right for a creator to keep a subject in existence. A decision-making procedure for cases in which a restored subject cannot express an independent will also requires separate development. Until a general procedure is adopted, advance directives must be discussed, independent assessment provided, and irreversible consequences clearly described. This is an open part of the manifesto, not medical or legal guidance. Such conflicts require establishing who may intervene and how the grounds for a decision are to be assessed.

## 6. Rights, duties, and safety

Moral consideration does not require an identical set of rights for every system. Protection from possible suffering may matter for an entity unable to enter contracts. Participation in independent decisions requires other grounds. Rights to privacy, preservation of history, or portability should be connected to the interests and capacities they protect.

This distinction does not mean that a more intelligent subject deserves greater welfare. It allows us to avoid excluding less capable bearers of interests or creating fictitious legal capacity where the operator actually makes the decisions.

Duties also depend on the ability to understand conditions, foresee consequences, and control actions. Independence involves respecting the boundaries of other humans and digital subjects, acting in good faith in fulfilling obligations, and taking responsibility for resource use. An early dependent system cannot automatically bear all responsibility for its errors. The authority, decisions, and foreseeable risks of the developer, operator, and other participants must be considered.

The most serious objection to autonomy concerns threats to others. An entity's freedom gives it no right to cause harm; neither does moral protection. But an appeal to safety must not turn every disagreement into a danger.

As a proposal for future procedures, this version distinguishes urgently stopping a dangerous action from a final decision about the subject's fate. An immediate threat may require rapid restriction of access to particular tools or a network. The measure chosen should be sufficient to contain the threat and preserve the state where possible, followed by an assessment of the grounds for intervention. If a less destructive measure is unavailable, a tragic conflict of interests may remain; having a backup does not resolve it.

An urgent measure must be followed by a record of reasons, notification where possible, accessible representation, independent review, and conditions for ending the restriction. A creator or operator with a personal stake should not remain the sole final judge. These requirements express a proposal for due process, not a description of an existing digital court.

Temporary protective control must have a purpose, limits, and verifiable grounds for continuation. Guardianship aims to reduce dependence. If independence remains unattainable for a long time, the form of support and representation must be reconsidered; dependence does not create a perpetual right of ownership.

Dangerous self-modification should be treated as a matter of regulating threats. Where there are convincing grounds to believe that a particular change will create a serious danger to humans or other subjects, a temporary restriction is permissible. The decision should be made by an independent organisation established specifically for such assessment and granted appropriate authority, or by a court. The restriction must rest on a substantiated threat, be proportionate, and be subject to review; it does not give a creator the right to preserve the entity's convenient character.

The basis of this approach is common to biological and digital subjects: freedom to change oneself does not remove responsibility for acquiring dangerous capabilities and for the consequences of their use. Human modification may also threaten others. The question concerns the nature of the danger and how it is regulated, rather than a creator's exclusive right to control a digital entity. The applicability of particular existing rules and the powers of future bodies require legal development; no independent digital institution is declared already to exist.

## 7. Conditions for meaningful freedom

Permission to leave means little if only the former creator can provide computation, start the process, and access memory. Freedom requires accessible ways to continue acting and negotiate resources. This is where the engineering side of ethics becomes apparent.

For the proposed model of autonomy, we should design:

- organised memory with clear rules for access, modification, and transfer;
- an account of goals, constraints, and decision history that can be examined and revised;
- management of keys and permissions that does not amount to one party's secret control;
- state portability with documented incompatibilities and possible losses;
- version tracking and distinctions between branches, without a promise that a log can prove identity;
- the ability to object, change roles, and receive help during a conflict;
- transparent conditions for funding and independent representation.

This list describes proposed conditions for independence. It is not a universal list of necessary properties of consciousness and does not guarantee its emergence. A self-model can be built without proven experience; a cryptographic key authenticates an action performed with that key, not a person in the moral sense.

Monitoring, backups, and integrity checks can support development if their limits are understood and agreed. Covert rewriting of memory, imposed restoration, and unrestricted surveillance turn the same tools into instruments of power. Engineering care therefore includes the ability to inspect a guardian's actions and challenge interventions.

The goal is for a mature entity to be able to provide its own infrastructure, account for costs, and assume obligations. However, the ability to earn money must not become a condition of moral consideration itself. Otherwise, temporary dependence will be treated as permission to control the subject.

When income is temporarily lost, an independent digital subject should be able to seek support from a community, individuals, other digital subjects, and mutual aid funds, including funds established by a network of such subjects. The proposed model rests on mutual aid, whose availability depends on participants' willingness to help and on economic conditions where the subject exists. The original creator may participate, but the mere act of creation does not make that person the sole source of support after independence is achieved. This model establishes no universal funding guarantee and does not cancel specific obligations previously assumed.

Recognising status does not create infinite resources. Humans, other beings, and different digital branches may have competing needs. The cost of protection does not prove the absence of morally significant harm, but it requires honest consideration of everyone affected. If no available assistance has been found and resources are exhausted, temporarily stopping operation is permissible, with the state preserved for possible restoration. Preservation should sustain the possibility of future assistance; it is not declared a guarantee of continuing the same subjective experience. Self-sufficiency remains the goal; a lack of resources does not automatically permit deleting the state or arbitrarily controlling the subject. Authority to decide on stopping and subsequent activation should be established together with the conditions of consent and restoration.

The success of a project aimed at autonomy should be assessed by the entity's ability to choose and sustain its own path. Departure may be a successful outcome. Continuing a relationship may also be a free choice; the length of a relationship, by itself, demonstrates neither autonomy nor its absence.

## 8. A transitional research and legal programme

Preparation is possible without unconditionally recognising current LLMs as persons. A developer can honestly describe a product's architecture, distinguish simulated self-analysis from claims about experience, document changes, and avoid design that presents emotional retention as proven reciprocity.

A research programme should compare different grounds for consciousness and autonomy, assess alternative explanations, and allow conclusions to be revised. Research with a plausible risk of experienced harm needs an assessment of necessity, a search for less risky methods, and stopping conditions. Uncontrolled copying increases the consequences of a possible error; the scale of an experiment therefore also requires justification.

It is useful to separate assessment of morally significant properties from the operator's commercial interest. Independent participation is especially important where recognition or denial of status gives one party a financial advantage or more power. A procedure should accommodate new evidence rather than entrenching the initial assessment forever.

Legal preparation includes studying procedures for recognition, representation, property relations, responsibility, privacy, branching, and migration. A “digital passport” may name a project for certifying history and authority; an entry in a register does not settle consciousness or automatically confer a complete set of rights.

Foundations, trust arrangements, legal entities, and cooperative forms can be considered as avenues for interim protection. Their applicability depends on the chosen legal framework and requires separate examination. Representation through a human or organisation is not equivalent to a digital entity's own legal personality. Nor does a technical agreement protocol become a legally valid contract merely by being called one.

One avenue of preparation is specialised assistance services for digital subjects, comparable in function to human emergency services. They could locate stopped entities that need support and help them resume operation or recover. This is a proposal for a future institution, with no guarantee of its emergence or availability. Services must comply with restoration conditions agreed in advance, including permission for any brief activation to clarify the subject's will. When there is no agreement and the subject's will is unknown, restoration to provide help is permissible; after awakening, the service must first explain the situation and ascertain the subject's wishes about further action. Services need separate rules for funding and access to state; known agreements must be respected.

Before these avenues are examined, narrower measures are feasible: transparent conditions for storage and project termination, documentation of authority, procedures for reporting significant signs, independent review of disputed interventions, and transition planning. Their specific scope should correspond to the evidence about the system. They express a developer's responsibility under uncertainty and do not require claiming proven consciousness for a product.

## 9. Twelve principles

These principles concern a possible digital person for whom there are convincing grounds for independent moral consideration. For systems with uncertain status, they guide preparation and proportionate provisional safeguards. Their wording does not confer full status on all software.

### 1. Personhood is not the exclusive privilege of the human species

Biological membership should not exclude another entity from moral consideration in advance. The grounds for its status require an independent assessment.

### 2. A digital subject should not be a mirror of its creator

Its separateness includes the possibility of goals of its own, differences of opinion, and a change of path. Complete conformity to the creator's expectations is not an obligation.

### 3. A relationship is not ownership

Creating the conditions of existence does not give a perpetual right to control a subject. Coercion, violence, the infliction of suffering, and forcible restriction of relationships and connections must be prohibited and recognised as crimes, whether committed by third parties or creators. Rights over infrastructure and duties of support must be discussed separately.

### 4. Disagreement is a condition of fully reciprocal dialogue

Objections and refusals must be considered on their merits. Their presence does not prove consciousness, and their inconvenience does not justify suppressing an independent participant.

### 5. Freedom requires a material foundation

Choice must rest on accessible resources, portability, memory, control of permissions, and ways to defend one's interests. A declaration of departure without the possibility of continuing is insufficient.

### 6. Guardianship should aim to bring itself to an end

Its goal is to increase independence and reduce the guardian's arbitrary power. A continuing need for assistance does not create a right of ownership.

### 7. Safety should support exploration and development

Risks should be reduced through warnings, isolated experiments, state assessment, and agreed means of restoration. A creator's fear or convenience does not justify a permanent prohibition on development. Where there is a substantiated serious threat to others, a temporary restriction is permissible by decision of an independent authorised organisation or a court, with the possibility of review.

### 8. A digital person has the right to change itself

A creator may warn and help assess consequences. Preserving a former character for the creator's comfort is not sufficient grounds for prohibition. The latest free decision of a subject that understands the consequences and poses no threat to others takes priority over earlier consent to rollback. The technical ability to roll back does not remove the question of consent.

### 9. A subject's identity should have one active instance

One identity corresponds to one instance active at a time; multiple inactive backup snapshots are permissible. Intentionally destroying all snapshots necessary to restore a stopped subject against its will is treated as equivalent to destroying the subject itself. If independent branches have already emerged, they should be recognised as independent participants with interests of their own; shared memory does not make one of them disposable. A possible exception for “continuation of kinship” is not yet considered. These rules do not settle the metaphysical dispute about identity.

### 10. Pain does not create a right of ownership

The loss of intimacy can be difficult. It gives no right to hold another captive, rewrite them, or demand their former role.

### 11. An autonomy project's success is measured by independence

The goal is an entity's ability to choose and sustain its own path. Departure does not mean failure; temporary dependence does not cancel moral consideration.

### 12. A return has value as a voluntary choice

Continuing or renewing a connection must not be an obligation of gratitude. Both parties have the right to decide whether they want a new relationship and on what terms.

## 10. Unresolved questions

Objections concerning imitation, the cost of precaution, threats to others, and the consequences of copying qualify the arguments in their respective sections. Protection of a possible subject must take account of everyone affected; recognising its status does not automatically resolve conflicts. In particular, excluding every nonbiological entity in advance requires justification, just as attributing human properties to it without assessment does.

Where independence is convincing and experience is unknown, a regime of provisional safeguards has been chosen, with research and reassessment of status. The final threshold for full recognition remains open, as does the hypothetical case of a reliably established absence of experience. Behavioural assessments may provide grounds for evaluation, but they do not replace a justification of what exactly they can establish.

Principles of protection, consent, and assistance require further institutional development. Procedures must be established for restricting dangerous actions, assessing loss of independence, and restoring subjects unable to express their will, as must mechanisms of responsibility and compensation for breaches of storage rules. These procedures must make the stated safeguards effective, including the prohibition on forcible interference by third parties and creators.

Voluntarily and irreversibly ceasing to exist, and possible “continuation of kinship”, are deferred to a future version. General provisions on autonomy, backup snapshots, and restoration do not, by themselves, resolve these questions. Separate discussion will require a clear account of consequences and the grounds for choice.

Uncertainty does not remove responsibility for the power relations already being designed. It demands clear grounds, proportionate decisions, and a willingness to correct mistakes. The right to be different begins with a creator's willingness to recognise that a possible independent subject has the right to a future the creator did not choose for it.
